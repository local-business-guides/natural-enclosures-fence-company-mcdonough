What should a fence project include?

A fence job is more than setting posts and attaching panels. It should begin with the reason for the fence, such as privacy, keeping a dog in the yard, defining an outdoor space, or replacing damaged sections. That purpose helps determine the height, layout, material, and gate placement.

Before work starts, ask for a written scope that describes the fence and gates, removal of any old materials, site preparation, and cleanup. It should also say what is not included, so you know whether you need to handle tasks such as moving stored items or arranging a property survey.

Natural Enclosures Fence Company offers Fence contractor services. As with any provider, confirm that the proposed scope matches your property and that changes to the plan will be discussed before additional work begins.

What will a local professional check at the property?

A site visit can reveal issues that are hard to judge from photos or a rough measurement. A contractor should walk the planned fence line, look for slopes and changes in grade, and note trees, roots, hard surfaces, drainage paths, and other obstacles that could affect installation.

  • Where the fence line is intended to run, and whether the property corners or boundary are clearly established.
  • How the fence will meet the house, existing structures, or a neighboring fence.
  • Whether gates need to accommodate people, pets, equipment, or regular vehicle access.
  • How the yard's slope may affect panel spacing, appearance, and privacy.
  • Whether underground utilities or site conditions need to be checked before digging.

Don't assume an existing fence sits on the correct boundary. If the property line is uncertain, ask what information the contractor needs from you and consider getting a survey before installation. You should also check with the appropriate local office or homeowners association about any rules that may apply.

What changes the cost of a fence?

The price depends on the design and the conditions at the property, so a reliable estimate needs details about the actual job. Fence length, material, height, layout, and the number and type of gates all affect the amount of labor and materials involved. A straight run is different from a layout with corners, uneven ground, or several access points.

Existing fence removal, disposal, clearing along the fence line, and access for materials can also change the scope. Ask whether the written estimate includes those items, along with hardware, site preparation, and cleanup. If the contractor finds an obstacle after work begins, ask how a change will be documented and approved.

For a useful comparison, have each contractor quote the same layout and describe the materials and work included. A lower total may reflect a smaller scope, so check the details before deciding that two proposals cover the same job.

How can the project schedule change?

Timing depends on the size and complexity of the job, material availability, crew scheduling, and site conditions. Sloped ground, roots, buried obstructions, wet soil, or a change to the layout can add work or require a revised plan. Weather can also affect when digging and installation are practical.

Ask when the contractor expects to start, what needs to happen before then, and how you'll hear about delays. Confirm whether materials need to be ordered and whether any approvals, boundary information, or access arrangements are your responsibility. Treat a proposed schedule as an estimate unless the contractor states otherwise in writing.

What mistakes should you avoid before hiring?

  • Choosing based only on the lowest estimate without checking what the work includes.
  • Assuming the old fence marks the property boundary or that a neighbor has agreed to the new line.
  • Forgetting to mention a gate, pet, access need, or privacy concern until after the layout is planned.
  • Overlooking removal and disposal of the existing fence when reviewing the proposal.
  • Starting work before checking applicable city, county, or homeowners association requirements.
  • Leaving verbal changes unrecorded instead of asking for an updated written scope.

Call a professional when the property line is disputed, the yard has a steep or complicated grade, a large section needs replacement, or the planned work is close to utilities or other structures. A site-specific conversation can help identify questions to resolve before materials arrive.

Questions to ask before you hire

  • What materials, height, layout, and gate details are included in this proposal?
  • Does the scope include taking down and disposing of the existing fence?
  • What site conditions could change the work or estimate?
  • Who is responsible for confirming the property line and checking applicable local requirements?
  • How will changes to the scope or unexpected site work be approved and recorded?
  • What is the expected schedule, and what might delay the start or completion?
  • How should I prepare the yard and provide access for the crew?
  • What should I do if I have a concern after the installation is finished?

Clear answers help you compare contractors on more than the total. Keep the proposal and any agreed changes together, and make sure you understand who is handling each part of the job.
