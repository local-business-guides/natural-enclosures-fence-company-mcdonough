What should a fence company cover?

A good contractor starts by asking what the fence needs to do. A boundary marker, a privacy screen, and a barrier for pets can call for different layouts, materials, gate locations, and levels of visibility.

The written proposal should describe the work clearly enough for you to compare it with another bid. Check whether it spells out measuring, materials, posts, gates, removal of an existing fence if needed, cleanup, and disposal. Ask about anything that is not clearly listed rather than assuming it is included.

Fence options compared by type

The right fence type depends on the job, the look you want, site conditions, and how much upkeep you're comfortable with. Ask the contractor which materials are available for your project and compare the full scope, not just the appearance.

  • Wood: A familiar choice for privacy and a traditional look. Ask about the specific wood, finish, maintenance, and how the design will handle slopes.
  • Vinyl: Often considered for a consistent appearance and lower routine upkeep. Confirm the available styles, color, repair approach, and how the material suits your site.
  • Metal: Options such as ornamental metal can provide an open view while marking a boundary. Check the layout, gate details, and whether it meets your privacy or pet needs.
  • Chain-link: A practical option when visibility and boundary marking matter more than privacy. Ask about height, coating, gates, and any screening options if you want more coverage.

Material names alone don't tell you what the finished project includes. Compare posts, gates, removal, cleanup, and any site preparation listed in each written proposal.

What does a local professional check before recommending a fence?

A site visit helps a contractor understand the ground and access before settling on a layout. They may look for slopes, soft or uneven areas, trees and roots, existing structures, and places where a gate needs room to swing. These conditions can affect both the plan and the work involved.

A careful contractor will also ask where the property line is believed to be and whether there are buried utilities or drainage concerns near the proposed fence. If the boundary is uncertain, do not rely on a fence or an old marker alone. Confirm the line before installation so the new fence does not create a neighbor dispute.

What changes the cost of a fence project?

There is no useful price to quote without knowing the property and the requested work. Cost can change with fence length and height, material, terrain, access for equipment, the number and style of gates, and whether an old fence needs to be removed. Special layout needs or difficult ground can also add work.

Ask each contractor to separate the proposed work from optional items and to identify exclusions. That makes it easier to compare bids on the same basis. If a quote is much lower than the others, ask what materials, preparation, disposal, or follow-up work may not be included.

What can affect the project timeline?

Scheduling depends on the contractor's workload, material availability, weather, site access, and how much preparation the yard needs. Locating utilities, confirming a property line, or waiting for an approval can also affect when work can start. A contractor should explain which steps need to happen before installation is scheduled.

Ask for the expected start window and what could change it, rather than relying on a verbal guess about a finish date. If the project has a deadline, mention it before accepting the proposal and ask how delays will be communicated.

Which local details should you settle before installation?

The rules that apply can depend on the property's location and neighborhood. Check with the relevant local authority and your homeowners association, if you have one, about permits, placement, height, materials, and any review process. Do not assume the same requirements apply to every property near McDonough.

Before work is approved, confirm the intended fence line with the people who own or manage the property. Also ask who will arrange utility marking and whether that step must be completed before digging. A clear plan for these details can prevent rework and avoidable disagreements.

What mistakes should you avoid when hiring?

  • Choosing from a short description or photo without discussing the actual yard and its slopes, access, and obstacles.
  • Comparing bids that use different materials or leave removal, gates, or cleanup out of the scope.
  • Treating a review score as a substitute for a written proposal and clear answers about your project.
  • Assuming a neighbor's fence marks the legal boundary or that verbal approval covers every local or association rule.
  • Agreeing to a start date before confirming access, utility marking, and any required approvals.

Call a professional when the boundary is unclear, the ground is difficult, or you are unsure how a gate or layout will work. Those are easier to address before materials arrive than after posts are set.

Questions to ask before you hire

  • What fence style and materials are included in this proposal?
  • Does the scope include measuring, old-fence removal, disposal, and cleanup?
  • How are gates, corners, slopes, and changes in ground level handled?
  • What site conditions could change the scope or cost after work starts?
  • Who will check the property line and arrange utility marking before digging?
  • Which permits, local rules, or association approvals should I confirm?
  • What is the expected scheduling window, and how will changes be communicated?
  • What payment schedule and written terms apply to this project?
