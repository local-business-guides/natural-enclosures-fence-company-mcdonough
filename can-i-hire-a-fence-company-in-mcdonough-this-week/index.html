What should a fence contractor check before giving a plan?

A useful site review starts with the fence’s purpose and condition. For a new fence, the contractor should discuss the boundary, approximate layout, gates, height, material, slope, and how the fence will connect to existing structures. For a repair, ask which parts can be fixed and whether other sections are likely to need attention soon.

The contractor should also identify practical checks that could affect the job, such as underground utility locations, access for equipment, and any local permit or association requirements. Confirm who will handle each check before work begins. A written scope should say what is included, what is excluded, and how changes will be approved.

What changes the cost and timing?

There is no useful price to give without knowing the site and the work. The total can depend on fence length, material, height, gates, terrain, access, removal and disposal of an old fence, and the amount of repair or preparation needed. A quote should describe these factors rather than offer a total with no explanation.

Timing can change for many of the same reasons, as well as crew availability, material availability, weather, utility marking, and required approvals. Ask whether the proposed date is for measuring, ordering materials, or installation. If a contractor says the work can happen this week, clarify what must be ready first and what could move the date.

Compare quotes only after checking that they cover the same fence length, material, gates, removal, cleanup, and any stated warranty. A lower total may reflect a narrower scope, while a higher one may include items another quote leaves out.

What questions should you ask before you hire?

  • Can you visit or provide an estimate this week, and what is the earliest realistic installation date?
  • What exactly is included in the written scope, and what would count as extra work?
  • Which materials, fence height, gate details, and finish are included in the quote?
  • Who will check property boundaries, utility locations, and any permit or association requirements?
  • Will you remove the existing fence and take away the debris, or is that separate?
  • Who will be on site, and who should I contact if the schedule changes?
  • What payment schedule and written warranty terms apply to this project?

Listen for clear answers rather than a rushed promise. If a detail is still uncertain, ask the company to note it in the quote so both sides understand what has and has not been agreed.

What mistakes can slow the job or cause disputes?

  • Treating a verbal estimate as a complete scope. Get materials, measurements, gates, removal, and cleanup in writing.
  • Assuming a fence line is correct because an old fence is already there. Confirm the boundary before construction.
  • Agreeing to a start date before checking approvals, utility marking, and material readiness.
  • Choosing on total price alone without comparing what each quote includes.
  • Paying without understanding the payment schedule, change-order process, and warranty terms.

A professional should be willing to explain what is known, what still needs checking, and who is responsible for each next step. If the answers are vague or the scope keeps changing, pause before signing.

When is it time to call a fence pro?

Call a contractor when you need a new layout, a gate that does not close, leaning or damaged sections, or a repair where the cause is unclear. A site assessment is also useful if the ground slopes, the boundary is disputed, or you are unsure whether a repair will hold.
